Prologue

The Anatomy of Uncertainty: Why Data Science Unites Finance and Human Language

Data science is fundamentally the discipline of quantifying and navigating uncertainty. In quantitative finance, that uncertainty is expressed as stochastic volatility—the second moment of price returns reflecting the collective risk, panic, and exuberance of global markets. In cognitive computing and natural language processing, that same uncertainty manifests as human sentiment—unstructured linguistic expressions reflecting whether consumers will embrace or resist generational technological shifts like electric mobility.

While financial returns deal in numbers and social sentiment deals in text, both domains share a structural truth: linear models inevitably break down when faced with complex human systems. Classical econometrics (such as ARCH and GARCH) struggle when volatility clusters experience non-linear regime shifts. Similarly, traditional rule-based text lexicons fail when confronted with regional nuances, sarcasm, and domain-specific anxieties.

To solve both challenges, this work grounds itself in the industry-standard CRISP-DM (Cross-Industry Standard Process for Data Mining) lifecycle:

CRISP-DM
Lifecycle Backbone: Understanding → Prep → Model → Eval → Deploy

Act I • Financial Econometrics

Forecasting the Storm: Deep Neural Networks vs. GARCH on the S&P 500

The financial paper, conducted between WWU Münster and the University of Twente, investigates a pivotal question in modern risk management: Can non-linear artificial neural networks surpass the decades-old econometrics gold standard (ARCH and GARCH) in forecasting realized volatility for the S&P 500?

Asset managers must balance the risk-reward trade-off. Measuring potential drawdown is not just an investment imperative; it is a regulatory requirement under frameworks like ESMA, PRIIPs, and IORP II to compute Value at Risk (VaR) and Expected Shortfall.

1. Mathematical Foundation: Realized vs. Implied Volatility

Financial volatility is the degree of variation in asset prices over time. In this study, we focus on Realized Volatility ($\sigma$), which measures actual historical price variation computed from high-frequency and daily return streams:

Realized Volatility Formulation
σ = √( Σi=1N ri2 )
where ri = ln(Pt / Pt-1) is the log return of the S&P 500 index on day i, and N is the window horizon.

In contrast to Implied Volatility (which is derived backward from option prices via Black-Scholes and represents market expectations), realized volatility serves as ground truth for evaluating forecast accuracy.

2. The Classical Econometric Benchmarks: ARCH & GARCH

Financial time series exhibit volatility clustering: periods of high volatility are followed by high volatility, and tranquil periods by tranquil periods. Robert Engle's ARCH (Autoregressive Conditional Heteroskedasticity) and Tim Bollerslev's GARCH(p, q) model this by conditioning current variance on past squared residuals and past variances:

GARCH(1,1) Conditional Variance
σt2 = ω + α1 εt-12 + β1 σt-12
Condition of stationarity: α1 + β1 < 1. While GARCH models mean-reverting shocks, its linear combination cannot capture asymmetric leverage effects or sudden structural breaks.

3. Deep Learning Architecture & Empirical Results

To capture non-linear market interactions, we engineered a multi-layer Neural Network pipeline taking multi-day lagged returns, volume shocks, and technical indicators over the 2010–2021 S&P 500 dataset.

Model Type Primary Strength Primary Limitation Error Metric (RMSE)
ARCH Univariate Classical Analytically tractable Slow to adapt; ignores lagged variance Higher Baseline
GARCH(1,1) Autoregressive Classical Models volatility persistence Assumes symmetric linear response to shocks Moderate
Deep Neural Network Universal Approximator Captures non-linear dependencies and interaction effects Requires hyperparameter tuning & regularization Lowest (Optimal Fit)

Evaluation was governed by Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE) and Mean Absolute Error (MAE):

Evaluation Loss Functions
RMSE = √( (1/n) Σi=1n (Pi - Oi)2 )  |  MAE = (1/n) Σi=1n |Pi - Oi|

The findings proved that neural networks capture regime-switching shocks (such as the 2020 pandemic volatility spike) significantly faster than linear GARCH models, enabling tighter Value at Risk estimation for quantitative portfolio construction.

Python Implementation: Volatility Engine

import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
from arch import arch_model
from sklearn.neural_network import MLPRegressor
from sklearn.metrics import mean_squared_error

def compute_realized_volatility(prices, window=21):
    """Compute 21-day annualized realized volatility from price series."""
    log_returns = np.log(prices / prices.shift(1)).dropna()
    realized_vol = log_returns.rolling(window=window).std() * np.sqrt(252)
    return realized_vol.dropna()

def train_volatility_forecasters(train_returns, test_returns):
    # 1. Classical Benchmark: GARCH(1,1)
    garch = arch_model(train_returns, vol='Garch', p=1, q=1)
    garch_fit = garch.fit(disp='off')
    garch_forecast = garch_fit.forecast(horizon=len(test_returns)).variance.values[-1]
    
    # 2. Deep Neural Network Regressor
    X_train = np.column_stack([train_returns[i:-5+i] for i in range(5)])
    y_train = train_returns[5:]
    nn_model = MLPRegressor(hidden_layer_sizes=(64, 32), activation='relu', max_iter=500)
    nn_model.fit(X_train, y_train)
    
    return garch_fit, nn_model
Act II • Master's Thesis Research

Decoding Global Perception: Multilingual EV Sentiment across 4 Languages

Supervised by Prof. Dr. Gottfried Vossen at the University of Münster, this research tackled a central obstacle in the green transition: What drives public sentiment toward electric cars, and why do adoption dynamics differ so fundamentally across the globe?

Rather than analyzing a single homogenous dataset, the pipeline engineered an end-to-end multilingual scraping and analysis tool covering four pivotal automotive markets:

  • Norway (NO): The world leader in per-capita EV adoption.
  • Germany (DE): Europe's automotive manufacturing heartland, navigating legacy transition.
  • China (CN): The largest EV market and global battery powerhouse.
  • English-Speaking World (EN): Global baseline across media and forums.

1. Lexicon-Based Baselines vs. Deep Transformers

The research first established a multilingual lexicon baseline (custom dictionaries, VADER, TextBlob). However, lexicon methods stumbled over negation, domain-specific terminology (e.g., "fast-charging" vs "battery degradation"), and cultural colloquialisms.

Switching to Transformers (BERT, RoBERTa, and multilingual XLM-RoBERTa) revolutionized classification accuracy. Bidirectional attention allowed the model to contextualize entire sentences, distinguishing between pure range anxiety (negative) and technological pride in charging network expansions (positive).

2. Empirical Regional Sentiment Distribution

The empirical results from Chapter 6 of the thesis revealed fascinating cultural and structural divergences:

🇳🇴 Norwegian (NO): Sustainability Pioneer 63.6% Positive | 24.1% Neu | 12.3% Neg
Key terms: bærekraftig (sustainable), miljø (environment), fornybar (renewable). Dominant fear: rekkeviddeangst (range anxiety).
🇬🇧/🇺🇸 English (EN): Balanced Technology & Cost 40.4% Positive | 26.9% Neu | 32.7% Neg
Positives: clean, efficient, performance. Negatives: infrastructure gaps, high upfront cost, battery lifespan.
🇨🇳 Chinese (CN): Smart Cockpits & Rapid Innovation 36.0% Positive | 47.5% Neu | 16.5% Neg
High focus on 智能 (smart tech), 环保 (eco-friendly), 快速 (fast charging), with low negative resistance (16.5%).
🇩🇪 German (DE): Engineering Scrutiny & Total Cost of Ownership 20.2% Positive | 52.3% Neu | 27.4% Neg
Cautious sentiment dominated by technical inquiry (52.3% neutral) and skepticism over kosten (cost), ladeinfrastruktur (charging network), and reichweite (range).
Act III • Generative AI & Adoption Modeling

Autonomous Likert Survey Engineering: Fine-Tuned T5 & BART

Standard sentiment analysis is passive: it reports what people have already written. To transform analysis into active research, the thesis pioneered an Autonomous Survey Generation Engine using fine-tuned T5 (Text-to-Text Transfer Transformer) and BART models.

1. Context-Engineered Prompt Retrieval

The system takes raw user-generated content (scraped forum threads, product reviews, news articles) and applies context-engineered prompt conditioning to formulate structured 5-point Likert Scale survey questions (from Strongly Disagree to Strongly Agree):

Prompt Engineering Pipeline for Survey Generation
Input Text → Context Conditioning → Fine-Tuned T5/BART → Grounded Likert Questions → Sentiment Verification

For example, from thousands of German discussions regarding highway speed limits and cold weather battery drain, the engine autonomously formulated targeted questions:

"On a scale of 1 to 5, to what extent does winter battery degradation influence your decision to purchase an EV over an ICE vehicle in Germany?"

2. Predicting Electric Car Adoption Patterns

The generated questions and responses were fed into supervised machine learning classifiers to predict consumer adoption intent. Among the evaluated algorithms (Random Forest, Decision Trees, Logistic Regression, and K-Nearest Neighbors), KNN achieved the highest predictive accuracy in categorizing consumer willingness to adopt based on regional sentiment vectors.

Python Implementation: Multilingual Transformer Sentiment Pipeline

from transformers import AutoTokenizer, AutoModelForSequenceClassification, pipeline
import torch

class MultilingualEVSentimentAnalyzer:
    def __init__(self, model_name="cardiffnlp/twitter-xlm-roberta-base-sentiment"):
        self.tokenizer = AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained(model_name)
        self.model = AutoModelForSequenceClassification.from_pretrained(model_name)
        self.classifier = pipeline("sentiment-analysis", model=self.model, tokenizer=self.tokenizer)
        
    def analyze(self, text, language="en"):
        """Classify sentiment across EN, DE, CN, NO with confidence scoring."""
        result = self.classifier(text)[0]
        label_map = {"LABEL_0": "Negative", "LABEL_1": "Neutral", "LABEL_2": "Positive"}
        return {
            "language": language,
            "sentiment": label_map.get(result['label'], result['label']),
            "confidence": round(result['score'], 4)
        }

# Example Regional Inference
analyzer = MultilingualEVSentimentAnalyzer()
print(analyzer.analyze("Elbiler er fantastisk bærekraftige for fremtiden!", language="no"))
print(analyzer.analyze("Die Ladeinfrastruktur auf der Autobahn ist noch viel zu teuer.", language="de"))
